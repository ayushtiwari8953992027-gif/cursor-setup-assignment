More info = more sales is a convenient lie. 

There’s a growing belief in sales and marketing that if a buyer just had enough information, they’d close themselves.

This is bullshit.

That’s not how buying decisions work. That’s how people want to believe they work—logically, clean, step-by-step. But it’s delusional!

People don’t buy based on logic. They buy based on EMOTION.

And when the stakes are high, buyers want certainty, trust, and leadership.

We’re living in a time where:

- Attention spans are collapsing
- Expertise is collapsing
- Certainty is not increasing with more data → it’s decreasing
- You can throw all of the possible information at a buyer, and they’ll still want to get on a call.

Why?

Because people don’t just want information.

They want energy. They want clarity. They want certainty. They want to talk to a human who can guide them.

They want someone who can say:

“Here’s what’s right for you. Here’s what’s not. I’ve seen this before. Let me help you make the right call.”

That’s not just selling. That’s leadership.

The future of sales belongs to people who can lead—not just pitch.

The reps who can show up with real expertise, real conviction, and say: “I’ve got you. Let’s figure this out together.” 

That’s the skill that’s becoming rare! 

If you’re in sales today: Double down on the one thing that AI, product tours, and slide decks will never replace—your ability to earn trust and lead people forward.

That’s the future. And it’s human as hell.

I’m going to say something you may not like: sales is not hard. 

It’s people who are difficult. 

I’ve been preaching this for years, and it’s never been truer than it is today. 

What makes sales feel hard isn’t the actual work. It’s not writing an email, picking up the phone, or asking for the close. 

It’s YOU.

Your doubts. Your hesitations. Your fear of rejection. Your ego. 

That’s what slows you down and leads to poor choices. 

Sales itself is not rocket science. There are jobs out there that are actually HARD. Sales is challenging, but it’s simple. 

The problem is discomfort. Every meaningful action in sales requires you to step outside your comfort zone. Following up one more time. TEN more times! Asking the blunt question. Calling when you’d rather procrastinate. 

And that’s why my biggest advice to you is this: Pain and discomfort are the signal. 

They’re the signal that you’re going in the right direction.

linkedin post 3
Subject: We Fucked Up

 That was the actual subject line of the email my co-founder Anthony sent to a prospect after we completely botched their trial experience.

Here’s what happened:

A great company was trialing our product. They had lots of technical questions for our team. But instead of stepping up and helping them succeed, we sent them straight to HANDOFF HELL.

One rep couldn’t answer their questions and passed them to someone else. Turned out that person was on vacation.

Another rep stepped in, but didn’t know the context.

There were slow replies, misunderstandings, and a total lack of urgency. But worst of all: we showed a complete disregard for the customer.

I noticed the lead in Close—the company name caught my eye—and I peaked at the notes to see the status of the deal. What I saw was unacceptable. Their trial had expired, they were frustrated, and we had handled their concerns poorly.

I did what many leaders do: I jumped into Slack, started asking our team “WTF happened?” and tried to conduct an internal investigation to fix the processes and systems that led to this catastrophe.

But Anthony, our co-founder and COO, didn’t just point fingers and play the blame game. He took responsibility.

He wrote the customer a very candid email with the subject line: “We Fucked Up.”

It got their attention.

Anthony sincerely apologized, restarted their free trial, and promised to personally help them if they gave Close a second chance.

They hopped on a call right away.

In the end, after having a few conversations with this company, our solution wasn’t the right fit—they had some custom technical needs that our product isn’t built for—but we helped them clarify what they needed to look for in a CRM and worked to get them one step closer to the best solution for them (even if it wasn’t ours).

We didn’t get the sale, but we salvaged the relationship. And sales is, above else, about relationships.

If you can’t win the deal, win back your integrity.

There’s a time to optimize and there’s a time to humanize. My instinct was to optimize our internal processes and Anthony’s was to do the human thing and take care of the customer first.

We all fuck up. It’s inevitable. Hell, even I fucked up in my response to the first fuck-up!

What matters is how you show up after the mistake.

You fucked up a sale? What's done is done. But don't give up. Fight for a second chance.

Next time this happens, we won’t start with Slack threads.

We’ll start by fixing the real problem: the customer's pain.

We want to say directly and honestly: “We fucked up. If you give us a chance, we'll make it right.”
